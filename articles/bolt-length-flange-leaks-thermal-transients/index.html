The Impact of Bolt Length on Mitigating Flange Leaks Under Transient Thermal Conditions

Flanged joints can leak when they see thermal shock or other transient thermal conditions. This article looks at how bolt length affects the flexibility of the joint, using Hooke's law, and how a longer effective bolt length helps the joint keep its preload.

By Baher Elsheikh. First published on LinkedIn, February 2024. 4 min read.

Introduction

Flange connections are critical to the integrity of pressure vessels and piping systems in many industries. One of the challenges these connections face is leakage under thermal shock and transient thermal conditions. The relationship between bolt length and joint flexibility, explained through Hooke's law, is central to understanding and reducing this risk.

Understanding thermal shock

Thermal shock occurs when a system goes through a rapid and large change in temperature. Flange connections are sensitive to it because it causes differential thermal expansion between the bolts and the flange. If this is not managed, it puts additional stress on the bolts and flanges and can lead to leaks.

Bolt length, strain and Hooke's law

Bolt length is a key factor in how much strain a flange connection sees during a thermal shock event. According to Hooke's law, within the elastic limit the strain of a material is directly proportional to the applied stress. Longer bolts, when chosen correctly, give the joint additional flexibility, so it can better absorb thermal expansion or contraction. The lower change in load reduces the risk of gasket failure and helps the overall integrity of the flange connection.

Material factor

A bolt material with a thermal expansion coefficient different from that of the flange material can have a real effect on the flexibility of the joint. Selecting materials with matching thermal expansion coefficients helps.

The modulus of elasticity of bolting materials decreases as their temperature rises. As a typical example, the modulus of an ASTM A193 B7 bolt drops by about 17% when the bolt temperature is raised from 70 °F (20 °C) to 800 °F (427 °C). Unless there are offsetting differential expansion effects (discussed below), the bolt preload decreases in the same ratio.

Selecting the bolt length

Temperature differential

The expected temperature differentials in operation must be assessed carefully. In line with Hooke's law, longer bolts can accommodate larger temperature differentials without loss of joint integrity.

In transient conditions, the flange can heat up faster than the bolts. The temperature difference causes different thermal expansion, and the flange displacement is forced onto the bolts. This effect is temporary, but the extra load can push the bolt stress into the plastic region of the stress-strain curve. The bolt is then permanently elongated, the clamping force is lower, and the chance of a leak is higher.

The relationship between the bolt preload and the change in bolt length can be estimated with Hooke's law:

Fp = (As · E / Le) · Δb

where Fp is the bolt preload, As the bolt stress area, E the modulus of elasticity, Le the effective bolt length and Δb the change in bolt length.

Increasing the effective bolt length Le reduces the change in bolt preload for the same change in bolt length Δb.

Tensile load is not applied to a bolt from end to end. It is applied between the inner face of the nut and the underside of the head (or the inner face of the other nut). The whole bolt is not loaded, although part of the head and the engaged threads in the nut carry some stress. Any length of bolt beyond the engaged threads of the nut does not contribute to the preload calculation.

Accommodating the joint components

The bolt length should allow for washers, nut height and the required thread protrusion.

For assemblies tightened by bolt tensioning, the bolt length should allow the threaded portion of the bolt to extend at least one bolt diameter beyond the outside nut face on the tensioner side of the joint.

Avoid excessively long bolts. Excessive thread protrusion can make disassembly of the joint difficult because of corrosion, paint or damage on the exposed thread.

Summary

Careful selection of bolt length, based on Hooke's law, is important in the design and maintenance of reliable flange connections, particularly where thermal shock and differential thermal expansion are expected. With appropriate bolt lengths, engineers and operators can reduce the risk of leaks and support the long-term reliability and safety of the system.
