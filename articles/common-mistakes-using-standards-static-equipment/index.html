Common Mistakes That Can Be Avoided When Using Standards for Static Equipment

Standards make many decisions simple, which is exactly why the same mistakes keep repeating. Six examples from piping and flange standards, and how to avoid them.

By Baher Elsheikh. 5 min read.

Standards are widely used for static equipment and are referenced for design, fabrication, inspection, repair, maintenance and integrity management. They are documents that establish engineering or technical requirements for products, practices, methods or operations, and in many cases they allow specific requirements to be selected directly, without intensive engineering work or calculation.

Even so, some mistakes in applying standards are seen again and again, and they can be avoided. The usual causes are:

  1. Unfamiliarity with the standard's content or structure. Users sometimes know only the tabulated values they need and do not check the other requirements or precautions in the same standard.
  2. Relying on copied publications. Much of the content of standards is reproduced in vendors' publications and on internet forums, but these do not necessarily include all the requirements of the standard. That can lead to errors.
  3. Overconfidence. Frequent use of similar standards can lead users to assume that a less familiar standard has no different requirements.
  4. The standard is not available to the person using it.
  5. Limited time and work done in a hurry.

Careful use of standards easily eliminates these mistakes and avoids their safety and economic consequences. The following examples show common mistakes that can be avoided.

1. Pipe schedules in ASME B36.10M and ASME B36.19M

ASME B36.10M (welded and seamless wrought steel pipe) specifies pipe thickness by schedule number, such as Sch. 40, Sch. 80 and Sch. 120. ASME B36.19M (stainless steel pipe) specifies it by a number followed by the letter S, such as Sch. 10S, Sch. 40S and Sch. 80S.

For relatively small pipe sizes, the schedules in the two standards correspond to the same thickness: for NPS 4, Sch. 40 and Sch. 40S are both 6.02 mm. For larger sizes this is no longer true. For NPS 16, Sch. 80S is 12.7 mm while Sch. 80 is 21.44 mm, about 1.7 times thicker. Specifying the wrong one by mistake makes a significant difference to the flow area and the weight of the pipe.

2. Outside diameter is fixed, inside diameter is not

The outside diameter of a pipe is fixed for each nominal size, while the inside diameter varies with wall thickness. If this is overlooked when sizing pipe, the result can be an undersized bore and a higher pressure drop than intended. Table 1 compares the flow area for the cases above.

3. A weld neck flange needs its bore specified

The information needed to specify an item fully can differ with the type of item. Slip-on, threaded, socket weld, lapped and blind flanges to ASME B16.5 can be specified by class (150, 300, 600 and so on), facing (RF, FF, RJ) and material. A weld neck (WN) flange also needs its hub bore, or the matching pipe schedule. If this is missed, the flange delivered may have a hub that does not match the pipe it is to be welded to.

4. Not every size exists in every class

Standards do not always follow a uniform pattern. ASME B16.5 gives flange dimensions for all pressure classes, but some sizes are not available in some classes:

Someone might specify an NPS 20 Class 2500 flange and be surprised to learn that it is not a standard flange.

5. Bolting strength categories in ASME B16.5

Some important requirements are easy to miss unless the standard is read carefully. One example is the classification of bolting materials in ASME B16.5 as low, intermediate and high strength. Low-strength bolts are limited to lower pressure classes and must not be used in high-rating applications.

Bolting materials with a specified minimum yield strength of no more than 206 MPa (30 ksi) are classed as low strength. These, and other materials of comparable strength, are to be used only in Class 150 and Class 300 flanged joints, and only with the gaskets described in ASME B16.5. Flanged assemblies with low-strength carbon steel bolts should not be used above 200 °C (400 °F) or below −29 °C (−20 °F). There are limitations on intermediate-strength bolts too; check ASME B16.5 for the details.

Take care when specifying stainless steel bolts: some, such as ASTM A193 B8A Class 1 (SS304), are classed as low-strength bolting.

6. ASME B16.47 Series A and Series B are not interchangeable

Large flanges (above NPS 24) to ASME B16.47 come in two series, A and B, which are not interchangeable. In general, Series A flanges are thicker, heavier and stronger than Series B flanges of the same size and class, so they can withstand more external load. Series B flanges need more, but smaller, bolts and have a smaller bolt circle diameter than Series A.

Make sure the whole flanged joint is of the same series: both mating flanges, the bolting, the gasket and any item in between, such as a flanged valve.
